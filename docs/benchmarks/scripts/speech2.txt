Translation studies has always been uneasy about the word equivalence. For decades scholars argued about whether a translated text could ever be equal to its source. Nida proposed dynamic equivalence, which shifted attention from the words on the page to the response of the reader. Later theorists found that unsatisfying because it assumed we could measure a reader's response at all. Toury turned the question around entirely and asked what actually functions as a translation within a receiving culture. That move gave the field its descriptive turn and it changed what counted as evidence. Instead of judging a translation against its original, researchers began mapping the norms that governed choices. Corpus methods arrived and made those norms countable for the first time. But counting brought its own problem, because frequency is not the same thing as significance. A word that appears four hundred times may tell you less than one that appears twice in a crucial passage. So the discipline now lives with two habits of mind that do not always agree. The interpretive tradition wants to read closely and the empirical tradition wants to measure widely.
